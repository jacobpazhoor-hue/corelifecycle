## The real hierarchy (VERIFIED — Wikipedia "Russian mafia" / "Thief in law", Oxford BJC "Factions
and Brokers in the Russian Mafia", RBTH "Thieves-in-law: Russia's close-knit criminal corporation",
Jamestown "Protection in Russia")

The bratva ("brotherhood") is the general term for a Russian/post-Soviet organized-crime cell; the
vory v zakone ("thieves in law" / "thieves-in-law") are its elite, code-bound aristocracy. Real,
commonly documented rank terms, low to high:

1. **Patsan / Shestyorka** — a prospect / errand-runner. "Shestyorka" literally means "the six" (the
   lowest card in a deck) — slang for the bottom rung, runs messages, does small theft, proves
   loyalty and nerve before anyone will vouch for him.
2. **Boyevik / Boets** — a soldier/enforcer. Does the actual violence: debt collection, beatings,
   "krysha" (protection) collection runs.
3. **Brigadir** — a brigade (brigada/crew) leader. Runs a cell of boyeviki, owns a route or a slice
   of territory, answers upward for its take.
4. **Avtoritet** ("authority") — a made, respected criminal figure with real standing and often a
   legitimate-front business (club, dealership, import firm) laundering the brigade's money.
   VERIFIED: traditionally distinguished from a vor — a vor rose through underworld ranks; an
   avtoritet historically could be an outside figure (official, "made" through connections/money)
   who never did the underworld apprenticeship, which is part of why purist vory looked down on them.
5. **Smotryashchiy** ("the one who watches / overseer") — put in charge of a whole city, district, or
   prison on behalf of the vory, collecting and forwarding tribute up the chain.
6. **Vor v Zakone** ("thief in law") — the crowned elite title, conferred by **koronatsiya** (a
   crowning ceremony) once a candidate (historically a shestyorka/patsan who's earned it) is proposed
   by a pakhan or senior vor and accepted by other vory. Bound by **ponyatiya** (the thieves' code):
   traditionally forbidden to marry/have a family, hold a legitimate job, own property, cooperate
   with or work for the state in ANY capacity (not even a prison job), or testify — and required to
   support other vory and the obshchak. VERIFIED (RBTH, Grokipedia "Thief in law").
7. **Pakhan** — the boss of a specific bratva/cell, its supreme day-to-day authority; usually himself
   a vor v zakone. Functions like a don/godfather for that organization.

The show's ladder (8 on-screen levels, low to high) maps this real hierarchy directly: Patsan ->
Boyevik -> Brigadir -> Avtoritet -> Smotryashchiy -> Vor v Zakone (koronatsiya) -> [running an
export/foreign cell] -> Pakhan. Level 7 (foreign cell) is grounded in the REAL case of Vyacheslav
"Yaponchik" Ivankov, a Solntsevskaya-linked vor sent to Brighton Beach, NYC in 1992 to take control of
Russian organized crime in North America (VERIFIED: multiple sources incl. Grokipedia "Solntsevskaya
Bratva") — used as background texture for "the network reaching abroad," not a claim this episode's
composite protagonist IS Ivankov.

## The obshchak (common fund) — VERIFIED (Grokipedia "Obshchak")
The obshchak is the organization's collective war-chest, tracing to Gulag-era vory in the 1930s.
Outside prison (svobodny obshchak), it's funded by **5–20% levies on gang profits** (theft, drugs,
racketeering) and pays for jailed members' families, legal defense, bribes, and enforcement. Inside
prison (lagerniy obshchak) it buys food/medical care/privileges and bribes guards. CRITICAL STORY
HOOK: money a vor handles is never legally "his" — the code requires it flow to the obshchak, not
into personal wealth. This is the show's spine: status and danger climb every level; PERSONAL money
never really does, because the code owns it before you do.

## Krysha ("roof") — the protection racket — VERIFIED, SHARE-WORTHY FACT
"Krysha" = Russian slang for protection: businesses pay a corrupt cop, official, security firm, or
gang monthly to avoid "problems." A Yeltsin-era administration analytical report found that by the
mid-1990s an estimated **70–90% of Russia's private enterprises and banks in major cities** were
paying tribute to organized crime, totaling **10–20% of turnover** (VERIFIED: Jamestown Foundation,
multiple corroborating sources). This is the "wait — that's real?" beat: nine in ten businesses paying
a tax that isn't a tax.

## Prison tattoos as a resume — VERIFIED (corrections1.com, RBTH, multiple tattoo-history sources)
Not decorative — a coded CV, and wearing an unearned one risks a beating or death:
- **Stars** on the knees: will never kneel to "musor" (police/authorities) — never cooperate.
  Stars on the chest/shoulders: higher rank, only for the most respected.
- **Epaulettes** (shoulder tattoos mimicking military rank insignia — captain/lieutenant/colonel
  motifs): signal criminal rank and contempt for the state's own uniformed authority.
- **Cathedral/church domes** on the chest: one dome per prison sentence served — a visual sentence
  count.
This episode's recurring sensory anchor motif is the **tattoo needle / buzzing hand-tattoo coil and
the smell of the ink** — re-triggered at every level-up as a new mark is earned/paid for in skin.

## Suchya voyna ("Bitch Wars") — the code's real historical fracture (flagged: order-of-magnitude/
general-history, not hard-sourced casualty figures in this pass)
After WWII, some vory broke the "no cooperation with the state" code by serving in the Red Army or
taking camp administrative work; purist vory who'd refused all cooperation considered this an
unforgivable betrayal of ponyatiya. Returning "suki" (bitches/traitors) and loyalists fought a bloody
internal purge inside the Gulag camp system in the late 1940s–50s. Used in this episode as the
historical root of the "old code vs. new code" rivalry beat (a modern echo, not a literal retelling).

## Modern erosion of the code (general/well-documented trend, not single-sourced)
Since the 1990s privatization chaos, a "new generation" of vory and avtoritety increasingly own
businesses, property, and families in practice — something purist thieves regard as illegitimate.
This episode uses that real tension as the mentor/rival axis: an old-school vor mentor who never
broke the code vs. a rival avtoritet who openly flouts it for money and visibility.

## Story spine for this episode
SPINE: WHAT THE CODE COSTS vs. WHAT IT PAYS — every promotion is measured in TWO numbers: the money
that passes through your hands (rubles/dollars, escalating — but flagged on-screen or in narration as
obshchak/organization money, not "yours"), and the number of things the code has taken from you
(family, your name, the right to own anything, eventually your life). Named want: Katya, a girl from
your block. Named mentor: Ded Grisha ("Grandpa Grisha"), an old vor v zakone who crowns you and dies
loyal to the code. Named rival: a modern avtoritet nicknamed "Zoloto" (Gold) who flaunts the wealth
the code forbids, and whose contempt for tradition is what starts the modern purge that costs Ded
Grisha his life. Sensory anchor: the tattoo needle / ink smell, re-triggered at every level.

## Verified numbers used for the escalating $ overlay (order-of-magnitude, flagged where estimated)
- Patsan: near-zero — a few hundred rubles for a run (~$5–10).
- Boyevik: a weekly krysha-collection cut, on the order of low hundreds of dollars/week.
- Brigadir: a crew's monthly skim, low five figures/month.
- Avtoritet: a laundering front's declared "revenue," high five to six figures/year personally
  visible, though most is not truly his.
- Smotryashchiy: overseeing a district's tribute — an aggregate flow in the low millions/year through
  his hands, funneled onward.
- Vor v Zakone (koronatsiya): the overlay INVERTS here — the crowning strips your personal accounts
  to near-zero (surrendered to the obshchak) even as the organization's flow around you is largest.
- Pakhan: nominal control over an obshchak in the tens of millions — never personally spendable
  without answering to the vory below him.
