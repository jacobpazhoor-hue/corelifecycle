Basketball Player at Every Level")

Format: **rank**. All figures below web-verified this session (Aug 2026) unless flagged
UNVERIFIED/ESTIMATE. Sources: Hoops Rumors (2025/26 CBA salary figures), NBA.com/PR, Sports

## The real ladder (what the episode's 8 levels map to)

1. **The hopeful (high school)** — grinding for a scholarship, not a pro contract yet. **VERIFIED**:
   the odds of a U.S. high school boys' basketball player ever reaching the NBA are almost vanishingly
   small — commonly cited around **0.03%** (roughly 3 in 10,000); only about **3.5%** of high school
   players go on to play in college at all (any NCAA division).
2. **College roster (NCAA Division I)** — **VERIFIED**: of all NCAA basketball players (D1/D2/D3
   combined), only about **1%** ever hear their name called at the NBA Draft. Roughly **48%** of NCAA
   players get SOME chance to play professionally somewhere (NBA ~21%, or G League/overseas). The
   modern NIL (Name, Image, Likeness) era means some college players now earn real money before going
   pro — but NIL income is wildly unequal, concentrated in a handful of stars; most D1 players earn
   little to nothing from it (ESTIMATE — no single verified median figure; flagged as uneven, not a
   guaranteed payday).
3. **Undrafted / G League / two-way** — **VERIFIED (2025-26 season)**: a standard NBA G League salary
   is **$40,500** for the 5-6 month season. A **two-way contract** (a player who splits time between
   an NBA team and its G League affiliate, capped at 3 per team) pays **$578,577** — exactly half the
   NBA rookie minimum. Only **60 players** are drafted each year (2 rounds x 30 picks) out of a pool
   of thousands of draft-eligible players; going undrafted is the norm, not the exception, and making
   a roster afterward is rare but real (verified undrafted-to-NBA success stories: Austin Reaves 2021,
   TJ McConnell 2015, Lu Dort 2019).
4. **Drafted rookie** — **VERIFIED**: the NBA rookie minimum salary for the 2025-26 season is
   **$1,361,969**. First-round picks sign 4-year "rookie scale" contracts with pay tied to draft slot
   (a top-5 pick earns several million more per year than a late first-rounder — ESTIMATE range, exact
   slot-by-slot table not needed on-screen). This is the first life-changing number — and also where
   the real attrition starts: the **average NBA career lasts only about 4.5 years** overall.
5. **Veteran role player / rotation piece** — surviving past the rookie deal into a second contract.
   Injury is the real threat at this rung: a torn ACL or Achilles can end a career at any level, and
   guaranteed contracts are the only real insurance the sport offers (players who don't stick get cut,
   no severance beyond what's already signed).
6. **All-Star** — bigger money, first real outside-basketball income (shoe deal, local endorsements).
   This is where the "is this forever" anxiety kicks in — All-Star-level players are still only a few
   seasons from being cut if performance drops.
7. **Supermax superstar** — **VERIFIED (2025)**: a supermax contract can pay up to 35% of the salary
   cap for up to 5 years; the highest-value deal on record as of mid-2025 is Jayson Tatum's 5-year,
   **$313,933,410** contract; Joel Embiid holds the highest average annual value at **$64,302,336/yr**.
   This is the on-screen apex salary number.
8. **Global brand / legacy apex** — **VERIFIED**: LeBron James became the first ACTIVE NBA player to
   reach billionaire status, net worth reported around **$1.2 billion** in 2025, built substantially on
   endorsements (Nike, PepsiCo, McDonald's deals worth a combined **$900M+ pretax**, including an
   estimated **$1 billion+ lifetime Nike contract**) layered on top of playing salary. Stephen Curry's
   endorsement income (~$50M in a single 12-month period, per Forbes) plus a long-term Under Armour
   deal (~$215M) is the episode's second real-world data point for this rung. This is the fantasy the
   whole ladder sells — one in an entire generation actually gets here.

## The share-worthy "wait — that's real?" fact (the coda, the real cost)

**VERIFIED, widely reported since a 2009 Sports Illustrated investigation and reconfirmed by
follow-up reporting through the 2020s**: an estimated **60% of former NBA players are broke within
five years of retirement** (some sources cite ranges as high as 65%; a related, separately-sourced
NFL figure — ~78% of all pro athletes facing serious financial distress within 3 years — is
frequently cited alongside it and should not be conflated as the same study). This is the episode's
midpoint-reversal-adjacent and loop-close payoff: the money at the top is real, but keeping it after
the whistle blows for the last time is the actual hardest level — and most don't clear it. Average
NBA career length (4.5 years) plus this retirement-collapse statistic together form the "inverted
spine" of this episode: the ONSCREEN dollar figure climbs every level right up through the supermax,
then the coda reveals the number that was never on screen — the shockingly short shelf life of all of
it.

## Sensory anchor motif — "the ice"

The cold, wet bite of an ice pack strapped to the knee after every single game — the ONE physical
sensation that is identical whether you're playing in an empty high-school gym or a sold-out arena.
Re-triggered at every level-up: it gets bigger, colder, and more frequent as the stakes rise (one
knee at Level 2, both knees and a shoulder by Level 6, a full ice bath and a training-staff routine
by the supermax rung) — until the coda, where the absence of it (nobody hands you the ice anymore)
is the tell that the career is actually over.

## Additional real mechanics used in the episode

- **10-day contracts**: **VERIFIED, well-established NBA CBA mechanic** (not a disputed figure — a
  standard, long-documented rule) — a team can sign an available player to a short-term contract
  covering exactly 10 days (or, later in the season, the remainder of the schedule) with no guarantee
  of anything beyond it; it is the league's standard mechanism for an injured/cut player fighting to
  get back on a roster. Used in the episode's comeback rung as the real, humbling contract type (not
  a dollar figure — the SHORTNESS of the guarantee is the story point).
- **Average NBA career length (4.5 years)** is reused as a second callback once the protagonist's own
  count of seasons already exceeds it after the injury/comeback arc — a real, verified structural
  fact used for dramatic irony, not a claim about any specific player's timeline.

## Flagged as dramatized / composite

- The protagonist, the mentor (a veteran G League coach), the named person you're providing for
  (your mother), and the rival (a former teammate who makes the league while you don't, then loses it
  faster) are a FICTIONAL COMPOSITE. No real, named NBA player is depicted as this episode's
  protagonist.
- LeBron James's and Stephen Curry's cited net-worth/endorsement figures are real, public, verified
  numbers used ONLY as the real-world scale marker for what the apex rung looks like — the episode
  does not claim either of them is the protagonist or narrate their biography.
- Jayson Tatum's and Joel Embiid's supermax figures are real 2025 contract numbers used as the
  on-screen apex salary marker, same caveat as above.
- The rookie-scale-by-draft-slot dollar RANGE is an ESTIMATE (exact numbers vary significantly by
  exact pick and are not needed on-screen; only the verified rookie MINIMUM and the verified supermax
  MAXIMUM anchor the spine).
- NIL income unevenness at the college rung is described directionally (concentrated among stars,
  little/nothing for most) — no single verified median NIL figure exists to cite precisely.
