## The real historical model (grounding, not depicted as fact)

- **H. Wayne Huizenga's actual 1962 start**: borrowed **$5,000** from his father-in-law, bought **one
  used garbage truck and 20 commercial accounts** from another small Broward County, FL hauler, and
  drove the route himself from 2 a.m. to noon before knocking on doors for new business in the
  afternoon. [VERIFIED — Encyclopedia.com, Waste360, Wikipedia] He later merged his growing operation
  with two other small operators (Dean Buntrock, Larry Beck) in 1968 to form **Waste Management, Inc.**,
  which took the company public in **June 1971**, raising **~$4M**, having by then rolled up **133**
  small independent haulers — it became the largest waste company in the world. [VERIFIED — multiple
  sources] This episode's protagonist, company, and every specific later dollar figure are a fictional
  composite BUILT ON this real shape (one truck → roll-up → public company), not a biography — $5,000
  in 1962 is roughly $50-55K in 2026 dollars (CPI-adjusted), which is the modernized Level-1 debt figure
  used below. [FLAGGED — CPI adjustment is an estimate, not an official BLS conversion for this specific
  figure]

## Real industry economics (the comp range powering the on-screen $ escalation)

- **New rear-loader garbage truck**: **$150K-$400K**, with **~$250K** a common price for a well-equipped
  25-30 yd³ unit; used trucks run well below that. [VERIFIED — Kademe/industry pricing 2025-2026]
- **Landfill tipping fees**: US average **~$62/ton** (2024, up ~10% YoY per EREF), ranging regionally
  from **~$35/ton (Kansas)** to **~$128/ton (Hawaii)**; Northeast averages **~$81/ton**, South Central
  **~$45/ton**. [VERIFIED — EREF 2024 National Tipping Fee Survey]
- **Roll-up acquisition multiples (2025-2026 market)**: sub-$2M-EBITDA owner-operator haulers sell at
  **3.0x-5.5x SDE**; $2-5M EBITDA regional platforms trade **5.0x-7.0x EBITDA**; $5-15M EBITDA platform
  candidates clear **6.5x-7.5x EBITDA** with no post-collection assets, **8.0x-9.5x** with a transfer
  station, and **9.5x-11.0x** with an owned, permitted landfill — owning disposal infrastructure is the
  single biggest valuation lever in the industry. [VERIFIED — CT Acquisitions/Kingdom Broker 2026 waste
  hauling valuation guides] A real cited comp: a mid-sized Southeastern hauler sold to a regional
  consolidator at **7.2x EBITDA (~$18.7M enterprise value)**. [VERIFIED, same source]
- **Active 2026 PE roll-up platforms** in this exact space (for texture/jargon, not depicted as this
  episode's buyer): LRS, Meridian Waste, WIN Waste, Coastal Waste — regional consolidators actively
  acquiring 20-50 independent haulers per platform. [VERIFIED — CT Acquisitions 2026 tracker]

## The share-worthy "wait, that's real?" beat

**Refuse and recyclable material collection is consistently one of the 4-5 deadliest jobs in America**
— a higher occupational fatality rate than police work. BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries:
**37.4 deaths per 100,000 full-time workers in 2024** (5th deadliest US occupation, behind only
logging, fishing/hunting, roofing, and structural steel work); **41.4 per 100,000 in 2023** (4th
deadliest that year); **22.6 per 100,000 in 2022** (7th deadliest). The leading cause is
**transportation incidents — workers struck or backed over by their own or passing vehicles**
(24 of the fatalities in 2023 alone). [VERIFIED — BLS National Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries,
reported via Waste Dive/SWANA/Resource Recycling, 2024-2026] This is the episode's core moral-cost
mechanism: a backing-fatality on a route where a safety/spotter-camera line item was cut to hit an
EBITDA target ahead of a sale is not a dramatic exaggeration — it is the single most common way this
real industry actually kills its own workers.

## The escalation ladder (levels + numbers appearing on screen)

The big gold number is the protagonist's **NET WORTH** (not company revenue) — the "$0 to billionaire"
spine the title promises; the sub-caption carries the **scale/cost** (trucks, counties, a body count),
never a second dollar figure, to keep the gap between "what you're worth" and "what it cost" legible.

1. **YEAR 1 — one truck, borrowed money.** -$52,000 net worth (the loan), one used truck, 20 accounts.
   Overlay: **-$52,000 · ONE TRUCK · 20 STOPS**
2. **YEAR 3 — the first real profit.** Debt cleared, a few trucks owned outright. Overlay:
   **$140,000 · SEVEN TRUCKS**
3. **YEAR 6 — the first roll-up, the first million.** Buying out tiny competitors on seller financing.
   Overlay: **$1,400,000 · 31 TRUCKS · THREE COUNTIES**

   EBITDA-multiple jump). Overlay: **$14,000,000 · THE LANDFILL YOU NOW OWN**

   cut costs before the deal closes. Overlay: **$68,000,000 · A BUYOUT OFFER**
6. **YEAR 13 — the reversal.** A backing-accident kills a longtime driver on a route where the
   spotter-camera budget was cut. Overlay: **-1 · MARCUS, 14 YEARS ON THE TRUCKS**
7. **YEAR 16 — OSHA, the fine, the recap closes anyway.** Overlay: **$410,000,000 · THE SALE CLOSES**
8. **YEAR 20 — the public company, the billion.** IPO / majority sale to a national platform. Overlay:
   **$1,200,000,000 · THE FIRST BILLION**

## Notes for the writer

- Keep the protagonist morally grey, not a cartoon villain: the cut corner (skipping the spotter-camera
  line item to protect margin before a PE recap) is a real, common industry cost lever, not a cartoonish
  evil act — the story should let the audience feel how ordinary the decision seemed at the time.
- The BLS fatality-rate stat is real and should land as the "wait, that's real?" beat *before* the
  accident (planted early, e.g. at a safety-orientation beat), so the midpoint reversal pays off a fact
  the viewer already has, rather than introducing it cold.
- The $5,000/one-truck/20-accounts real Huizenga anchor should read as "inspired by," never named or
  claimed as biography — no real person's name, company, or specific year should be asserted as this
  episode's literal history.
- FLAG explicitly in narration/description that the protagonist, company, driver, and every dollar
  figure past Level 1's real-world-modeled debt are a fictional composite; the industry economics,
  multiples, tipping fees, and the BLS fatality data are real and independently documented.
