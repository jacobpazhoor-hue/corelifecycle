# Pirate — Golden Age of Piracy (~1650–1730) research

Scope: the Atlantic/Caribbean "Golden Age of Piracy," roughly the Buccaneering Period (1650s–1690s)
through the Post-Spanish-Succession Round/final flourish (1715–1726). Grounded in well-documented
figures (Bartholomew Roberts, Henry Every, Captain Kidd, Blackbeard, the 1718 Nassau pardon), the
1721 Roberts Articles, and historian Marcus Rediker's mortality/demographic research (*Villains of
All Nations*, *Between the Devil and the Deep Blue Sea*). Numbers marked VERIFIED are widely
corroborated across multiple sources; PLAUSIBLE/ESTIMATE are historian estimates with real spread —
flagged inline, not presented as exact.

## The real rank ladder (a pirate ship's chain of command)

Pirate ships were run as **elected, contractual democracies** — unusual for the era, and a strong
"wait, that's real?" beat in itself. Every man who signed the ship's Articles got one vote.

1. **Green hand / new recruit** — freshly signed on (often a merchant/navy sailor who "went on
   account" after a capture, or a volunteer from a port town). No share until Articles are signed.
2. **Able seaman / "private" crew** — a full crew member, entitled to **1 share** of any prize under
   the Articles.
3. **Specialists** — quartermaster's mates, gunner, boatswain, carpenter, master (navigator),
   surgeon. VERIFIED (Roberts' 1721 Articles, as recorded by Captain Charles Johnson): specialist
   officers drew **1.25–1.5 shares**.
4. **Quartermaster** — VERIFIED: the crew's elected second-in-command and the real seat of day-to-day
   power. Distributed food and loot, settled disputes, could countermand the captain on prize
   decisions, and led the boarding party in battle. Drew **1.5–2 shares**. On many ships the
   quartermaster — not the captain — held the real authority except during a chase or a fight.
5. **Captain** — VERIFIED: elected by crew vote, and could be **voted out** (deposed) for cowardice,
   cruelty, or mismanaging prizes — several real captains were, including on Roberts' own ships before
   he took command. Absolute authority ONLY during combat/chase; otherwise bound by the Articles like
   everyone else. Drew **2 shares** (occasionally more by separate crew vote for an especially prized
   captain).
6. **Commodore / fleet commander** — rare. Bartholomew Roberts at his peak (1721–22) commanded a
   small squadron of 3–4 ships simultaneously (VERIFIED, e.g. the *Royal Fortune* plus consorts) —
   the closest real analog to a "pirate lord" rank above a single captaincy.
7. **The Crown above all of it** — no pirate ever outranked the Royal Navy, the Admiralty courts, or
   a king's pardon. This is the "final rank" the ladder pays off: the reckoning.

## The money spine — real share values and the crash

- **Ordinary sailor's legal wage (merchant/navy, for comparison):** ESTIMATE, well-corroborated
  range, roughly **£1–£2.50/month** (~£15–£30/yr) in the early 1700s — brutal, often withheld, and
  the core economic reason men "went on account."
- **"No purchase, no pay":** VERIFIED — the pirate wage system. No salary at all; crew were paid
  **only** out of captured prizes, split by share.
- **Compensation for injury:** VERIFIED (Roberts' Articles and similar articles from other Golden Age
  crews, per Johnson's *General History of the Pyrates*): a real, itemized "workers' comp" schedule —
  loss of a limb or right hand paid roughly **600–800 pieces of eight** (or an equivalent number of
  enslaved people, a horrifying real detail worth flagging rather than glorifying), a lesser wound
  paid proportionally less. Genuinely one of the most "wait, that's real?" facts of the whole topic.
- **A landmark real haul — Henry Every, 1695:** VERIFIED core event: Every's crew captured the Mughal
  ship *Ganj-i-Sawai* off India. Total value estimates range **£325,000–£600,000** (contemporary
  accounts vary; treat as ESTIMATE with real spread) — one of history's most profitable pirate raids.
  Reported ordinary-crew shares are commonly cited around **~£1,000 per man** (ESTIMATE, some
  primary-source variance), against a legal sailor's annual wage of ~£20–30 — i.e. a single prize
  could pay **decades** of legal wages in one split. Every himself vanished with a captain's/leader's
  multiple share and was never captured; the manhunt for his crew was one of the first global
  criminal manhunts in history (VERIFIED).
- **Bartholomew Roberts' career scale:** VERIFIED — over roughly 1719–1722, Roberts and his crews
  captured an estimated **400+ vessels**, the highest tally of any Golden Age pirate. Individual prize
  values varied hugely (a small sloop vs. a loaded slave ship or Portuguese treasure convoy); no
  single reliable "total" figure exists, so the show should use PER-PRIZE share swings, not a single
  aggregate number.
- **The crash:** VERIFIED structural fact — pirates rarely kept wealth. Prize money was mostly spent
  fast in port (drink, gambling, women, debts) between voyages, or seized entirely on capture (Crown
  confiscation), or lost with the ship. There is no well-documented case of a Golden Age pirate
  "retiring rich" at scale — Every is the rare exception (successfully vanished; wealth's fate
  unconfirmed). This is the dual-spine drama: shares can spike into a life-changing sum, then crash to
  **$0** at the gibbet, exactly like the Ottoman/Roman apex-cost pattern.

## The Republic of Pirates — Nassau, 1706–1718

VERIFIED: a real pirate-governed settlement at Nassau, New Providence (Bahamas), effectively outside
any crown's control for roughly a decade — a genuine "pirate haven" with a rough, real self-governance
culture. Ended by **Woodes Rogers**, appointed royal governor, who arrived in 1718 carrying **King
George I's Act of Grace** — a royal pardon for any pirate who surrendered and swore off piracy.
Hundreds surrendered (VERIFIED); many later returned to piracy anyway ("went upon the account" again)
once the money ran out — a strong causal beat for a MIDPOINT-adjacent "the pardon didn't save you"
reversal, or an ironic side detail.

## The end — real execution facts

- **Captain Kidd**, hanged at Execution Dock, London, **1701** (VERIFIED). His body was afterward
  gibbeted (hung in a cage) at Tilbury Point on the Thames as a warning to sailors — VERIFIED, a real
  and visually strong image.
- **The largest mass pirate execution in history:** VERIFIED — after Bartholomew Roberts was killed in
  battle off Cape Lopez in **February 1722**, his surviving crew were captured and tried at Cape Coast
  Castle. **52 men were hanged** — the single largest mass execution of pirates on record. This is the
  single strongest share-worthy "wait, that's real?" apex fact for this episode (parallels Ottoman's
  "44 of 284 strangled").
- **Career mortality (Marcus Rediker, historian, ESTIMATE but widely cited in academic pirate
  history):** a Golden Age pirate's active career, once "on the account," averaged only a **few
  years** before death in battle, disease, shipwreck, or the gallows — commonly cited that the large
  majority of active Golden Age pirates (rough historian estimates cluster around **70%+**) did not
  survive to retire. Present this as a historian estimate, not a precise census figure.
- **"Walking the plank" is NOT well-documented as a common real pirate practice** — it's largely a
  later (18th–19th century) literary/theatrical embellishment (Robert Louis Stevenson-era popular
  fiction), not a verified Golden Age execution method. AVOID using it as a real beat; use documented
  methods instead (marooning — real and verified; hanging; being turned over to a colonial Admiralty
  court).
- **Marooning:** VERIFIED real punishment under many ships' Articles — abandoning a crew member alone
  on a remote island/sandbar with minimal supplies (sometimes a pistol with one shot, by tradition) as
  discipline for theft, cowardice, or violating Articles. Strong texture beat, distinct from
  execution.

## Jargon glossary (for narration texture)

- **"Going on account"** — VERIFIED period phrase for turning pirate / signing the Articles.
- **Articles** — the ship's written contract: shares, discipline, injury compensation, voted on and
  signed by the whole crew before a voyage.
- **Piece of eight** — VERIFIED, the Spanish silver dollar (real de a ocho), the dominant coin of the
  era and prize accounting.
- **The Jolly Roger** — VERIFIED generic name for pirate flags; designs varied by captain (Roberts
  reportedly flew a flag depicting himself standing on two skulls labeled ABH/AMH — "A Barbadian's
  Head" / "A Martinican's Head" — after those colonies' governors hunted him; a genuinely wild, real
  detail).
- **Careening** — beaching a ship to scrape the hull clean of barnacles/weed for speed — a real,
  frequent, unglamorous maintenance beat that also made a ship briefly defenseless.
