# Research: Lottery Winner (scenario format — "POV: You Win the Lottery")

Format = `scenario`. Not a job ladder — an escalating DECISIONS/$ ladder across a single life-changing
event and its aftermath. Levels are TIME + DECISION points (the moment you buy the ticket → the moment
you check the numbers → the claim → the spend → the family fallout → the lawsuit/curse → the rebuild →
the years-later apex), not ranks. Spine idea: the GOLD overlay carries the ADVERTISED jackpot at the
moment of the win, then the ACTUAL running net worth (which craters, not just grows) as decisions cost
you — mirrors the "paper vs. spendable" gap validated on `trillionaire` but inverted: here the number
peaks early (at the win) and the drama is how far and fast it falls, then whether/how much comes back.

## Real mechanics [VERIFIED via web search, 2026-07-30]

- **Odds**: Powerball jackpot odds are **1 in 292,201,338** ($2/ticket, current rules). Mega Millions
  jackpot odds are **~1 in 290,472,000** at $5/ticket under the current price/format. [VERIFIED —
  Powerball official prize chart / multiple 2026 news sources.]
- **Lump sum vs. annuity**: the "cash option" lump sum is roughly **50–60% of the advertised (annuitized)
  jackpot**, commonly cited around **~52%**, paid immediately as one payment — this is literally the
  cash the lottery has on hand, invested to fund the annuity. The annuity option instead pays the FULL
  advertised number as **30 graduated payments over 29 years, each ~5% larger than the last**. [VERIFIED —
  Kiplinger/lottery calculators, 2026.] This lump-sum haircut, BEFORE any tax, is the first big gap
  between the advertised number and what you can actually spend.
- **Taxes**: the IRS withholds **24% automatically** at the moment of payout, but a jackpot-size win
  lands you in the **top 37% federal bracket** — meaning another ~13 points is owed at filing, a bill
  many winners don't plan for. State tax varies widely; several states (California among them) do not
  tax lottery winnings at the state level at all, while others take 5–10%+. [VERIFIED — Kiplinger 2026.]
  Net effect used for this episode's math: roughly **only ~35–45% of the ADVERTISED jackpot ever becomes
  spendable cash** once the lump-sum haircut and taxes both apply.
- **2026 texture (real, current)**: as of late July 2026 the Powerball jackpot sat at **$663M with a
  cash value of $290.4M** before tax — a real, live example of the advertised-vs-cash gap. [VERIFIED —
  2026 news coverage.] Historical record jackpots for scale: the **$2.04 billion Powerball** won in
  November 2022 (single ticket, California) is the largest lottery jackpot in world history; Mega
  Millions' record is **$1.602 billion** (Aug 2023, Florida). [well-documented public record, not
  re-verified via search this session — treat as textured background, not load-bearing.]
- **Anonymity**: roughly half of US states let a winner claim through a trust or otherwise stay
  anonymous; the rest require the winner's name to be made public record — a real, usable plot lever
  (the choice to hide vs. be found). [general public-record fact, not independently re-verified this
  session — used as texture, not a specific cited number.]

## The "curse" — what's rigorous vs. what's myth [VERIFIED]

- The number everyone repeats — **"70% of lottery winners go broke"** — is **not a real, sourced
  statistic**. It is most often mis-attributed to the National Endowment for Financial Education (NEFE),
  and **NEFE itself has stated on the record it never produced or can confirm that figure.** This is the
  episode's central "wait — that's real?" reversal: the myth people repeat isn't real, but the actual
  research is quieter and scarier in a different way. [VERIFIED — NEFE public statement, 2018, resurfaced
  2026 reporting.]
- The real, peer-reviewed finding: Hankins, Hoekstra & Skiba, *"The Ticket to Easy Street? The Financial
  Consequences of Winning the Lottery"* (Review of Economics and Statistics, 2011), studied Florida
  lottery winners and found winners of **modest prizes ($50,000–$150,000)** were **MORE likely to file
  for bankruptcy within 3–5 years** than the general population — and that the extra risk was
  concentrated among winners who were **already financially precarious before they won**. Winning does
  not fix a shaky foundation; it just moves the collapse a few years down the road, and gives it a
  bigger radius. [VERIFIED — peer-reviewed study, MIT Press / Review of Econ & Statistics.] Notably: a
  2018 Swedish-lottery study (Lindqvist, Östling, Cesarini) found NO increased bankruptcy risk for large
  prizes there — the US pattern isn't universal, it's a US-specific mix of taxes, no wealth safety net,
  and family/social pressure. Used in-episode as the grounded, unglamorous version of "the curse."
- Secondary (aggregator-reported, not independently verified this session — flagged): a commonly
  repeated figure attributed to the Florida lottery commission puts **~23% of Florida winners of
  $1M+ prizes filing bankruptcy within 5 years** in the 2000s. Used only as soft texture in narration,
  never as an on-screen cited number.

## Real documented "curse" cases (real people — used ONLY as grounding texture/dialogue color, never
depicted as the protagonist; the protagonist, family, and every named character in this episode are a
FICTIONAL COMPOSITE)

- **Jack Whittaker** — won a **$314.9 million Powerball jackpot in December 2002** (West Virginia; the
  largest single-ticket jackpot in the world at that time), took the lump sum. In the following years:
  robbed repeatedly (once drugged at a strip club and had **over $500,000 stolen from his vehicle**),
  his home and offices were broken into multiple times, he was sued repeatedly, his marriage collapsed,
  and in **2004 — about two years after the win — his 17-year-old granddaughter Brandi Bragg, to whom he
  gave $2,000 a week, was found dead of a drug overdose**, her old friendships eroded by the money. Five
  years on, he told reporters: **"I wish I'd torn that ticket up."** [VERIFIED via search — Washington
  Post/multiple 2026 retrospectives.]
- **William "Bud" Post III** — won **$16.2 million in the Pennsylvania Lottery in 1988**; within a year
  was reportedly over a million dollars in debt from bad investments and family lawsuits; his own
  brother was later convicted of hiring a hitman in a plot to kill him and his wife for a share of the
  winnings; filed for bankruptcy in the 1990s and died in 2006 largely broke. [well-documented in
  contemporaneous press; not independently re-verified via search this session.]
- **Abraham Shakespeare** — won **$30 million in the Florida Lotto in 2006** (took the ~$17M lump sum);
  was befriended by a woman, Dorice "Dee Dee" Moore, who gained control of his remaining money and
  **murdered him in 2009**; his body was found in 2010 buried under a concrete slab. [well-documented in
  contemporaneous press; not independently re-verified via search this session.]

These three cases are the real-world basis for this episode's "curse" beats (a robbery, a family
overdose, a predatory "advisor" who gets too close to the money) — dramatized and recombined into a
fictional story, never presented as biography of any one person.

## Episode design notes

- **Format**: scenario. Levels are TIME/DECISION points, not job ranks: the ticket → the numbers match
  → the claim (lump sum vs. annuity, name vs. trust) → the first spend → the family ask → the "curse"
  reversal (a predatory advisor + the exact mechanism of the real cases above) → the lawsuit/rock bottom
  → the rebuild → years later, the apex fact.
- **Spine**: gold BIG number = the running SPENDABLE net worth (peaks early at the lump-sum-after-tax
  cash-in-hand figure, then craters at the reversal, then partially recovers) — an inversion of the usual
  "climb" spine, since this story's shape is a spike and a crash, not a ladder. Sub-caption tracks the
  ADVERTISED jackpot once (to show the haircut) and then the more damning stat (bankruptcy filings, the
  bankruptcy-study finding, family member count asking for money).
- **Named people**: Dale (the gas-station clerk, six years of Friday tickets — the warm ordinary-world
  anchor, never a villain); Priya Chen (the wealth advisor who arrives after the win — introduced as help,
  her motives ambiguous until the reversal); a sister, Renn, and her son (the named want in Level 1 — a
  concrete unpaid medical bill, not "poor").
- **Unresolved universe thread**: an unclaimed fifth ticket from your old warehouse-shift lottery pool —
  a rumor that someone forged a claim on it — never resolved this episode, left for a future one.
