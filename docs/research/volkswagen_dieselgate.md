# Research: Volkswagen "Dieselgate" Emissions Scandal

Topic slug: `volkswagen_dieselgate`. All facts below verified via web search 2026-09-02. Every year,
figure, named person and quotation used in content.py must trace to this file per docs/BIBLE.md §2
and the prepublish audit rules (R1-R5).

## The mechanism, plainly

Volkswagen marketed a line of diesel cars in the US under the "Clean Diesel" banner, powered by
turbocharged direct-injection (TDI) engines, claiming they were both fuel-efficient and low-emission.
To pass US nitrogen-oxide (NOx) emissions tests while still delivering the fuel economy and
performance VW had promised, engineers built "defeat device" software into the engine control unit.
The software could detect it was being tested — using signals including steering-wheel position,
vehicle speed, engine operation, air pressure and the length/duration of the drive cycle, all of which
differ between a lab dynamometer test and real-world driving (a steering-angle-recognition feature was
authorized around April 2013 to sharpen this detection). When it detected test conditions, it switched
on full emissions controls. Under normal driving, those controls were turned down, and the cars emitted
NOx — a pollutant linked to smog and respiratory illness — at up to 40 times the legal US standard.

## Timeline

- **2009–2015**: model years in which roughly 11 million defeat-device-equipped diesel vehicles were
  sold by Volkswagen worldwide (VW Group brands: Volkswagen, Audi, Porsche, Skoda, Seat), including
  about 590,000 in the United States (the EPA's original September 2015 notice covered about 482,000
  four-cylinder 2.0-liter cars; a second EPA/CARB notice in November 2015 added roughly 10,000 3.0-liter
  six-cylinder Audi/Porsche/VW diesels, and the total US figure used in the DOJ's 2017 plea agreement is
  "some 590,000 vehicles").
- **2013**: The International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT), a nonprofit research group,
  granted West Virginia University's Center for Alternative Fuels, Engines and Emissions (CAFEE)
  about $69,000 to road-test real-world emissions from diesel cars sold in the US, hoping to show
  European regulators that clean diesel technology worked and could be adopted in the EU too. WVU
  researchers built a portable emissions-measurement rig for the trunk of a car, connected to a probe in
  the exhaust pipe, and drove the vehicles roughly 1,300 miles on real roads.
- **May 2014**: The WVU/ICCT study tested three vehicles — a VW Jetta, a VW Passat, and a BMW X5 (used
  as a working control). The BMW passed. The two Volkswagens exceeded the legal NOx limit by 15 to 40
  times during real-world highway driving. Results were reported to the California Air Resources Board
  (CARB) and the EPA.
- **September 3, 2015**: After more than a year of being pressed by CARB/EPA for an explanation of the
  discrepancy, Volkswagen engineers admitted to regulators that the cars contained a defeat device.
- **September 18, 2015**: The EPA publicly issued a Notice of Violation of the Clean Air Act against
  Volkswagen. EPA Assistant Administrator Cynthia Giles said: "Using a defeat device in cars to evade
  clean air standards is illegal and a threat to public health."
- **September 20–21, 2015**: Volkswagen issued a public apology. At a Volkswagen of America event in
  New York on September 21, 2015, VW America CEO Michael Horn told the audience: "Our company was
  dishonest, with the EPA and the California Air Resources Board, and with all of you. And in my German
  words, we have totally screwed up."
- **September 22, 2015**: Volkswagen admitted the software was installed in about 11 million diesel
  vehicles worldwide. The company's stock price fell by roughly a third over the following days.
- **September 23, 2015**: CEO Martin Winterkorn resigned after nearly nine years running Volkswagen
  Group (since January 2007). His resignation statement read in part: "I am shocked by the events of
  the past few days. Above all, I am stunned that misconduct on such a scale was possible in the
  Volkswagen Group," and: "I am doing this in the interests of the company even though I am not aware
  of any wrongdoing on my part."
- **September 25, 2015**: Porsche chief Matthias Müller was named Volkswagen Group's new CEO; he served
  until April 12, 2018.
- **October 8, 2015**: Michael Horn testified before the US House Energy and Commerce Subcommittee on
  Oversight and Investigations. Asked about the defeat device, he told Congress: "It was installed for
  this purpose."
- **September 2016**: VW engineer James Liang, a German national who had worked on the diesel engines
  at the center of the scandal, pleaded guilty to conspiracy — the first Volkswagen employee to do so —
  and agreed to cooperate with US prosecutors.
- **October 25, 2016**: A US federal judge approved a $14.7 billion settlement between VW, the DOJ, the
  FTC, the state of California and a consumer class action — at the time the largest auto-scandal
  settlement in US history — covering buybacks/fixes for roughly 475,000–500,000 2.0-liter US vehicles,
  environmental remediation funding, and consumer compensation.
- **January 7, 2017**: Oliver Schmidt, former head of VW's US environmental and engineering compliance
  office, was arrested by the FBI in Miami while vacationing with his wife, as he attempted to fly back
  to Germany. He was charged with conspiracy to defraud the US, commit wire fraud, and violate the Clean
  Air Act.
- **January 11, 2017**: The DOJ announced Volkswagen AG had agreed to plead guilty to three criminal
  felony counts and pay $4.3 billion in criminal and civil penalties ($2.8 billion criminal, $1.5
  billion civil/EPA), and that six VW executives and employees had been indicted in connection with the
  conspiracy (most in Germany, outside US jurisdiction, plus Oliver Schmidt).
- **March 2017**: Volkswagen formally entered its guilty plea in US federal court in Detroit and was
  sentenced under the plea deal, including three years of probation and an independent corporate
  monitor.
- **August 2017**: Oliver Schmidt pleaded guilty to the charges against him.
- **August 25, 2017**: James Liang was sentenced to 40 months in federal prison, two years of
  supervised release, and a $200,000 fine.
- **December 6, 2017**: Oliver Schmidt was sentenced to seven years (84 months) in federal prison and a
  $400,000 fine.
- **May 3, 2018**: A federal grand jury in the Eastern District of Michigan indicted former CEO Martin
  Winterkorn on four counts — one count of conspiracy to defraud the United States and violate the
  Clean Air Act, and three counts of wire fraud. Attorney General Jeff Sessions said: "If you try to
  deceive the United States, then you will pay a heavy price." Winterkorn has never faced this US case
  in person; Germany does not extradite its own citizens, and he has remained in Germany.
- **June 2018**: Audi CEO Rupert Stadler was arrested in Germany and held in pretrial detention for
  about four months — the first Volkswagen Group board-level executive to be jailed over the scandal.
- **September 2020**: Stadler and three co-defendants went on trial in Munich on charges specific to
  Audi's role in the scandal.
- **June 2023**: Stadler pleaded guilty at the Munich trial and was sentenced to a suspended term of one
  year and nine months in prison, plus a $1.2 million fine.
- **By September 2020**, Volkswagen itself had reported the scandal cost the company more than €31.3
  billion (roughly $34.7 billion at contemporary exchange rates) worldwide in fines, buybacks,
  settlements and legal costs — separate from, and larger than, the roughly $20 billion US-only total
  ($14.7B 2016 settlement + $4.3B 2017 DOJ settlement + smaller 3.0-liter settlements).
- **September 3, 2024**: Martin Winterkorn's own German criminal trial finally began, in Braunschweig —
  nine years after he resigned — on charges of fraud, market manipulation (failing to disclose the
  scandal to investors in a timely way) and making false statements to a German parliamentary inquiry
  in 2017. He denied all charges through his lawyer.
- **May 2025**: A Braunschweig court convicted four other former VW managers who had been on trial
  alongside Winterkorn's case: Jens Hadler (former head of diesel engine development) was sentenced to
  4 years and 6 months; Hanno Jelden (former head of powertrain technology) was sentenced to 2 years and
  7 months; two more-senior defendants received suspended sentences of 1 year 3 months and 1 year 10
  months respectively.
- **2025**: Winterkorn's own trial, tried separately from the other four due to his health, was
  suspended by the Braunschweig court after starting in September 2024, over an unspecified health
  issue. As of the most recent reporting found, no verdict in his case had been reached.
