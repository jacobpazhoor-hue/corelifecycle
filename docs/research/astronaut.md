# Research: Astronaut — the real NASA career ladder (for "Your Life as an Astronaut at Every Level")

Format: **rank**. All figures below are real and web-verified this session (Aug 2026) unless flagged
UNVERIFIED/ESTIMATE. Sources: NASA.gov, NASA OIG reports, SpacePolicyOnline, Space.com, ABC News/NASA
Mishap Investigation Board coverage, Wikipedia (cross-checked against NASA history pages).

## The real ladder (what the episode's 8 levels map to)

1. **Applicant** — a civilian (engineer, scientist, military/test pilot, physician) who applies to the
   open NASA astronaut call. **VERIFIED**: the 2024 astronaut selection cycle drew **more than 8,000
   applicants**; only **10 were chosen** — roughly a 0.125% acceptance rate (lower than any Ivy League
   school). The 2025 class (announced Sept 22, 2025, NASA's 24th astronaut class) was the first in NASA
   history where women outnumbered men (6 women, 4 men).
2. **Astronaut Candidate (ASCAN)** — **2 years** of training at Johnson Space Center, Houston: T-38
   supersonic jet flight training, spacewalk (EVA) training in the **Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory** (the
   NBL — the world's largest indoor pool, ~6.2 million gallons, ~40 ft deep, built to simulate
   weightlessness), robotics, Russian-language instruction (Soyuz/ISS ops), survival training, ISS
   systems. All new astronauts start as **GS-12, Step 1** — base pay **$100,287/yr (2025)**.
3. **Astronaut (unassigned)** — candidacy complete, but a flight assignment is NOT guaranteed and the
   wait can run **years** (historically 5-10+, worse after the Shuttle retired in 2011 shrank the U.S.
   seat count). Some selected astronauts never fly at all. Pay scales toward GS-13.
4. **First flight — ISS crew member** — a ~6-month rotation to the International Space Station. A
   Crew Dragon seat costs NASA roughly **$55 million** (2024 NASA OIG figure); the old Soyuz seat ran
   NASA **$70-90 million** in the 2010s. The Space Shuttle's per-launch marginal cost is most often
   cited around **~$450 million** (ESTIMATE — total program cost amortized per flight runs far higher,
   figures vary widely by methodology; flag as approximate in the episode).
5. **Veteran / spacewalker (EVA specialist)** — multiple flights, qualified for EVAs. **VERIFIED, the
   real near-fatal incident this episode's midpoint draws on**: on July 16, 2013, Italian ESA astronaut
   Luca Parmitano's helmet filled with **1-1.5+ liters of water** mid-spacewalk (a clogged cooling-loop
   filter), flooding his eyes, nose, ears, and mouth; Mission Control aborted the EVA and got him back to
   the airlock before he lost consciousness. NASA's Mishap Investigation Board later found the crew's
   calm, methodical response is likely what saved his life. (The episode's protagonist is a FICTIONAL
   COMPOSITE experiencing an EVENT OF THIS TYPE — not a claim this is Parmitano's life story.)
6. **Mission/Expedition Commander** — commands the ISS for a rotation, responsible for a 6-person crew
   and the station itself. ISS's total program cost across its international partners is most commonly
   cited around **~$150 billion** (ESTIMATE — partner-nation contributions vary by source/methodology).
7. **Chief Astronaut Office / program leadership** — manages the astronaut corps, decides who flies,
   grounds people for medical/performance reasons — the first real moral-cost beat with no villain, just
   institutional math.
8. **Artemis-era commander, reaching for the Moon** — the apex. **VERIFIED, and this is the episode's
   strongest "wait — that's real?" beat**: NASA's Artemis II flew a **crewed lunar flyby** (Reid Wiseman,
   Victor Glover, Christina Koch, CSA's Jeremy Hansen) that launched, flew 695,081 miles farther than any
   humans in history, and splashed down April 10, 2026 — the first humans near the Moon since Apollo 13
   (1970). It did **not land**. As of this writing (Aug 2026), NASA's own current plan pushes the actual
   crewed **landing** to **Artemis III (late 2027, orbital-only per the newest replan) with the landing
   itself now targeted for Artemis IV/V in 2028** — dates that have already slipped repeatedly (2025 →
   2026 → 2027 → 2028). **No human has stood on the lunar surface since Apollo 17, December 1972 —
   already over 50 years.** The episode's protagonist reaches for a LATER, still-hypothetical Artemis
   landing mission — grounded in the real program and its real delays, not a claim that a specific
   NASA mission or named astronaut is this protagonist.

## The pay/power gap (the on-screen spine)

Real astronaut base pay is a **fixed federal GS salary** — NASA confirmed in 2025 it pays astronauts
**$152,258/yr** (2024 pay schedule), most sit at GS-12/13/14. This is the "inverted spine" (see
[[inverted-spine-validated]] in ops/improvements.json — already proven on soldier/spy): personal pay
stays capped in the low-six-figures for an entire career, while the program/mission dollars moving
around the astronaut explode — a Crew Dragon seat ($55M) → the ISS program (~$150B) → a single SLS/
Orion Artemis launch (**$4.1-4.2 billion per launch**, NASA OIG-verified) → the whole Artemis campaign
(**~$93 billion through FY2025**, NASA OIG's own headline figure, released Feb 2024 and reconfirmed in
later coverage; NASA itself disputes the OIG's bundling methodology but doesn't dispute the >$80B scale).

## Sensory anchor motif — "the smell of space" (both halves are real, verified)

- **In orbit (EVA half)**: astronauts can't smell anything IN their suits (interior just smells
  plastic-y), but the instant they crack the helmet back in the airlock after a spacewalk, they report a
  sharp, distinctive smell — most describe **hot metal / spent gunpowder / seared steak / welded metal**.
  NASA's own explanation: atomic oxygen sticks to the suit and tools during the EVA, then reacts with
  oxygen flooding back into the airlock to form ozone (plus PAHs from other reactions) — a real, verified
  phenomenon (NASA's own Dec 2021 "fact of the month," multiple astronaut accounts).
- **On the Moon (the apex payoff)**: multiple Apollo astronauts independently reported the same thing
  about lunar dust tracked back into the cabin — Buzz Aldrin: "a pungent metallic smell, something like
  gunpowder, or the smell in the air after a firecracker has gone off"; Apollo 17's Jack Schmitt: "everyone's
  instant impression... was that of spent gunpowder"; Apollo 16's Charlie Duke called it out live to
  Houston. The leading explanation: lunar dust is chemically "unweathered" (no water/air erosion for
  billions of years) and oxidizes fast once exposed to a humid, oxygen-rich cabin.

## Real fatality/danger facts (for the danger-escalation, cautionary, not glamorized)

- **Training deaths**: NASA's T-38 Talon supersonic jet trainers have killed multiple astronauts over
  the decades — most notably, on Feb 28, 1966, Gemini 9 prime crew **Elliot See and Charles Bassett**
  died when See's T-38 clipped a McDonnell Aircraft building on a bad-weather instrument approach in St.
  Louis; both died instantly. (Real historical event — referenced as the TYPE of real risk astronaut
  candidates train under, not attributed to this episode's fictional composite mentor as if they were
  See/Bassett.)
- **In-flight program fatalities**: Apollo 1 (ground test fire, 1967, 3 dead — not in-flight), Soyuz 11
  (1971, 3 dead, cabin depressurization on reentry), Space Shuttle Challenger (1986, 7 dead, launch
  breakup) and Columbia (2003, 7 dead, reentry breakup) — 17 in-flight deaths across the whole era of
  human spaceflight, out of several hundred people who have flown. UNVERIFIED EXACT current fatality
  RATE (source figures for "total humans flown" vary 600-700+ depending on how suborbital commercial
  flights are counted) — the episode uses these as named historical program losses, not a precise
  percentage.
